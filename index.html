Why are some birds studied more than others?

In 2025, a team of ornithologists counted every scientific paper written about 293 North American birds between 1965 and 2020.

What the image shows

The birds that stay vivid are the well-studied ones. The Tree Swallow has 597 papers to its name. Other birds fade in step with how little has been written about them. The Gray Flycatcher has four papers, and the Canyon Towhee three.

Some birds have no papers at all. The Crissal Thrasher, the Philadelphia Vireo and eight of the names around them disappear completely.

Meet, for the first time

The Crissal Thrasher

Toxostoma crissale

Length
about 30 cm
Weight
53–70 g
Breeding population
about 82,000
Research papers, 1965–2020
none

A big, shy thrasher of the American Southwest and Mexico, living in dense brush along desert streams and in mesquite thickets. It spends most of its life out of sight, foraging on the ground under cover and digging in the desert soil with its long, curved bill. It would rather run than fly.

You are more likely to hear one than see it: its rolling calls carry at dawn and dusk. Pairs may stay together on their territory all year round.

Why “Crissal”? The name comes from the crissum, the patch of feathers under a bird’s tail. In this thrasher it is a warm chestnut, the brightest thing about it.

About these graphics

Paper counts come from Fischer, Otten, Lindsay, Miles and Streby (2025), who counted publications on 293 North American birds from 1965 to 2020 and found that research attention tracks how colourful and familiar a bird is. For the full analysis, read the paper or explore their data.

Population figures are breeding estimates for the USA and Canada from the Partners in Flight Population Estimates Database (version 3.0, 2019). Papers are a 56-year total and populations a snapshot from about 2006 to 2015, so the comparison is illustrative. The Scrub-Jay population combines four species that were grouped together in the original study. Neither abundance nor body size is the “right” way to share attention: rarity and conservation need draw research too.

Body lengths are from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology’s All About Birds identification pages; the Scrub-Jay uses the Woodhouse’s and California Scrub-Jay range. In the body-size state, each bird’s painted area is proportional to the square of its length.
